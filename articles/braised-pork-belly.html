Braised Pork Belly (Hong Shao Rou): The Chinese Comfort Classic

Updated October 2026 · 8 min read

Hong shao rou is the glossy, mahogany-red pork belly that anchors every Chinese home-cooked banquet — so tender the fat melts on your tongue. The magic is a long, gentle braise in soy, sugar, and spice. No wok tossing, no special equipment, just patience.

What "Hong Shao" Means

"Hong shao" literally means "red-cooking": meat braised in soy sauce and sugar until it turns a deep, lacquered red. The technique appears all over Chinese cooking, from rich stews to celebratory mains, and the result is always the same — savory, slightly sweet, and intensely glossy. Pork belly is the classic cut, though hong shao works beautifully with beef, chicken, and even tofu.

Ingredients

  • 800 g (about 1.75 lb) pork belly, skin on, cut into 1.5-inch cubes
  • 2 tbsp cooking oil
  • 3 tbsp rock sugar or brown sugar
  • 3 tbsp soy sauce
  • 1 tbsp dark soy sauce (for color — optional)
  • 2 tbsp Chinese cooking wine (or dry sherry)
  • 2 slices ginger, 4 cloves garlic, 2 star anise, 1 cinnamon stick
  • 2-3 cups hot water

Need to know your soy sauces? Our soy sauce guide breaks down which one to reach for.

Step 1: Blanch the Pork

Put the pork cubes in cold water, bring to a boil, and simmer 3-4 minutes. Drain and rinse — this removes the scum and tightens the meat so it doesn't fall apart later. Pat completely dry with paper towels.

Step 2: Caramelize the Sugar

This is the heart of hong shao rou. Heat the oil and sugar in a dry wok over medium heat, stirring constantly, until the sugar melts and turns a deep amber — not brown, not burnt. Immediately add the pork and toss to coat every cube in the caramel. The color you build here is the color of the final dish.

Step 3: Add Aromatics and Liquid

Add ginger, garlic, star anise, and cinnamon, then the soy sauces and wine. Pour in enough hot water to just barely cover the pork. Bring to a boil, then drop to a low simmer.

Step 4: The Long, Slow Braise

Cover and simmer gently for 60-90 minutes, until the pork is tender and the fat is translucent. Uncover, turn the heat up, and reduce the sauce until it's thick and glossy, clinging to each piece. Taste and adjust — a pinch of salt if needed.

Step 5: Rest and Serve

Let the pork rest 5 minutes off the heat so the sauce settles. Serve over steamed rice with blanched greens; the sauce is the best part, so don't skimp on it. Hong shao rou only improves overnight — leftovers are even better the next day.

Three Tips for the Glossy Finish

  • Don't rush the caramel — pale sugar means pale pork. Watch the color, not the clock.
  • Hot water only — cold water shocks the braise and toughens the meat.
  • Simmer, never boil — a rolling boil breaks the belly apart before it's tender.

Hong shao rou is patience rewarded. It takes over an hour, but almost all of it is hands-off — and the first time you fork a piece and the fat dissolves on your tongue, you'll understand why it's called China's comfort food.
