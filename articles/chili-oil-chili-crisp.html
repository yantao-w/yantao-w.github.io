Chinese Chili Oil & Chili Crisp: Make the Pantry Staple at Home

Updated November 2026 · 7 min read

A jar of good chili oil is the most useful thing in a Chinese kitchen after soy sauce. It's what makes dumpling dipping sauce sing, turns plain noodles into lunch, and gives cold cucumbers their punch. Store-bought jars are fine — but homemade is fresher, cheaper, and lets you decide how hot, how garlicky, and how crunchy it gets. The whole process takes about twenty minutes and one saucepan.

Two Different Things: Chili Oil vs. Chili Crisp

  • Chili oil (辣椒油) — the classic red oil: chili flakes steeped in hot oil, often with whole spices. Mostly liquid, intensely aromatic, and used as a flavor base or a finishing drizzle.
  • Chili crisp (油泼辣子 / 脆油辣子) — chili oil with stuff in it: fried garlic, shallots, sesame seeds, peanuts, sometimes fermented soybean. Crunchy, chunky, and eaten by the spoonful on rice, eggs, or noodles.

The same base recipe can go either way. Start with the oil, then decide whether to add the crunchy bits.

The Chili Flakes Matter Most

Supermarket "crushed red pepper" is usually the wrong tool: too fine, too stemmy, and dull in color. Look for coarse Chinese chili flakes — often sold as caizhi or erjingtiao flakes — which are fruity, deeply red, and only moderately hot. They're what gives authentic chili oil its color rather than just its burn.

A blend is best: mostly mild, aromatic flakes for color and flavor, plus a spoonful of a hotter variety for kick. All-hot flakes make oil that's painful but tasteless.

Ingredients (Makes About 1 Cup)

For the oil

  • 250 ml (1 cup) neutral oil — rapeseed, peanut, or canola
  • 4–5 tablespoons coarse Chinese chili flakes
  • 1 tablespoon sesame seeds
  • 3 slices fresh ginger, 2 scallions cut into lengths, 3 garlic cloves (left whole, smashed)
  • 1 star anise, 1 small cinnamon stick, optional 1 teaspoon Sichuan peppercorns
  • 1 teaspoon salt, 1/2 teaspoon sugar
  • 1 teaspoon black vinegar (optional, deepens the red color)

For the crisp (optional)

  • 3 tablespoons finely minced garlic
  • 3 tablespoons finely minced shallot or onion
  • 2 tablespoons crushed roasted peanuts

Method

  1. Prep the jar. Put chili flakes, sesame seeds, salt, and sugar in a heatproof bowl or jar. Mix well — dry flakes scorch unevenly if they're clumped.
  2. Warm the oil with aromatics. Put the oil, ginger, scallion, garlic, star anise, cinnamon, and Sichuan peppercorn in a cold saucepan. Heat gently over medium-low. The aromatics should sizzle softly and slowly turn golden — about 6–8 minutes. If they brown fast, the oil is too hot.
  3. Fish out the solids. Once the scallion edges are golden and the garlic is pale gold, remove everything with a slotted spoon. Let the oil cool for 1–2 minutes off the heat.
  4. Pour in three stages. Pour a third of the oil over the flakes and stir immediately — this wets everything and prevents burning. Wait 30 seconds, pour the next third and stir. Pour the rest, stir again. Staged pouring gives a redder oil and a deeper toasted flavor.
  5. Finish. Stir in the black vinegar if using — it fizzes and pushes the color toward deep red. Let the jar sit uncovered until fully cool, then seal.
  6. For chili crisp, fry the minced garlic and shallot in a little extra oil until just golden and crisp (about 3 minutes), drain, and stir them into the cooled oil with the crushed peanuts.

Temperature: The One Thing to Get Right

Oil that's too hot scorches the flakes into bitterness; oil that's too cool never extracts the aroma. The sweet spot is roughly 150°C / 300°F when it hits the flakes — hot enough to sizzle loudly, not hot enough to smoke. If you don't have a thermometer, drop a pinch of flakes into the oil: a gentle burst of bubbles and a slow reddening means you're there; an instant dark brown means back off and wait a minute.

Keep every utensil bone dry. A single drop of water in hot oil will spit violently, and wet flakes steam instead of toast.

How to Use It

  • Dumpling dip — chili oil, black vinegar, soy sauce, minced garlic. See our black vinegar guide for the ratios.
  • Noodles — a spoonful over plain boiled noodles with soy and scallion is a five-minute meal.
  • Cold dishes — smacked cucumbers, shredded chicken, blanched greens.
  • As a base — it's the shortcut to the red, aromatic top layer of mapo tofu and many Sichuan stir-fries.

Storing It Safely

Chili oil keeps well because oil is a poor environment for most microbes — but fresh garlic and shallot are the exception. Homemade oil containing raw or lightly cooked fresh aromatics should be refrigerated and used within about two weeks. For a longer-lasting jar, either use only the strained, aromatic-infused oil, or make the crisp with thoroughly fried (crisp) garlic and shallot. Always use a clean, dry spoon, and never leave the jar somewhere warm.

Make one jar this week and you'll find it disappearing faster than you expect — into dumplings, noodles, fried rice, and, inevitably, straight off the spoon.
