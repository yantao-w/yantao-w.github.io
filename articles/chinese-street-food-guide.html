Chinese Street Food: 8 Iconic Snacks You Can Make at Home

Updated October 2026 · 9 min read

The best Chinese food often isn't in restaurants — it's eaten standing up, on a paper plate, with steam rising into a night-market glow. Here are the street snacks Chinese locals actually queue for, plus home-kitchen versions that don't need a giant griddle or a fryer.

1. Sheng Jian Bao: Pan-Fried Soup Dumplings

The Shanghai classic that beats xiaolongbao for many locals. A thick, chewy bun with a crispy golden bottom and a spoonful of hot broth inside. Home version: use a simple pork and gelatin-stock filling (or just plenty of seasoned jelly), pleat the buns, then fry in a covered pan with a little water to steam, and crisp the bottoms at the end. Serve with black vinegar.

2. Jianbing: The Breakfast Crepe

China's most famous breakfast — a thin mung-bean crepe spread with egg, crispy wonton crackers (baocui), scallions, cilantro, and a swipe of sweet bean sauce and chile paste, folded hot. At home, a regular non-stick pan works: whisk a mung-bean-and-flour batter, pour it thin, crack an egg on top, flip, sauce, and roll. The crunch of the baocui is non-negotiable.

3. Chuan'r: Cumin Lamb Skewers

Xinjiang-style grilled lamb skewers, dusted heavily with cumin and chile powder — the smell that pulls you toward every night market. Home version: thread lamb shoulder onto skewers, grill or pan-sear fast and hot, then toss with a bold mix of cumin seed, ground cumin, chile flakes, and a pinch of sugar and salt. Serve immediately, still sizzling.

4. Tanghulu: Candied Hawthorn Skewers

The winter street classic: bright red hawthorn berries (or strawberries, or mandarin segments) dipped in a hard sugar syrup that shatters like glass when you bite. At home, boil sugar with a little water until it reaches the "hard crack" stage (about 300°F), dip the fruit, and let it set on parchment. The sweet-tart contrast is the whole point.

5. Baozi: Steamed Filled Buns

Steamed buns stuffed with pork-and-scallion or red bean paste, eaten by the bagful from steamer carts. They're easier than they look — a soft, slightly sweet dough, pleated around the filling, steamed 12 minutes. See our dumpling guide for the same wrapping instincts, then scale up.

6. Grilled Cold Noodles (Liangpi)

Northwestern cold noodles with a translucent, chewy texture and a slick of chile oil, garlic water, and vinegar. Restaurant-grade liangpi takes washing gluten from dough, but the cheat version uses rice noodles or wide wheat noodles dressed the same way — chili crisp, sesame, cucumber, and a punch of garlic. Shockingly close for 20 minutes of work.

7. Egg Waffle / Egg Puff

Golden, crisp-on-the-outside, custardy-on-the-inside street snacks from small iron molds. A home waffle iron (or a dedicated egg-puff mold if you get hooked) with a custard-style batter delivers the same caramelized edges and soft center. Best eaten hot, straight off the iron.

8. Roujiamo: The "Chinese Hamburger"

Shredded braised pork stuffed into a crispy flatbread — Xi'an's answer to the burger, and one of the most loved street foods in the country. Home version: braise pork belly with star anise, ginger, and dark soy until fall-apart, then stuff into warm pan-toasted bao or flatbread with cilantro and chile. See our pork dishes for braising basics.

Where the Flavor Comes From

Most street snacks lean on a handful of core seasonings — chili crisp, black vinegar, soy sauce, and cumin. Once you have those in the pantry, most of these recipes are just technique. If you're unsure which soy sauce to grab, our soy sauce guide sorts out light vs. dark in two minutes.
